# Professional interview guide

## Format

Use this as a flexible 20–30 minute guide, not a script or sales pitch. Ask permission to take
notes, explain attribution/anonymity choices, and begin with the participant’s current workflow.
Do not show scores, candidate locations, or private research material.

## 1. Context and current workflow — 6–8 minutes

1. Could you briefly describe your role and the kinds of heritage or archaeology projects you
   work on?
2. Walk me through how you evaluate archaeological potential for a large development area.
3. Where, if at all, do LiDAR, DTM, or other terrain products enter that workflow?
4. Who reviews terrain, and what tools or GIS workflows do they use?
5. Roughly how much terrain or how many project areas might be reviewed in a typical case?
6. What evidence and decisions come before and after terrain review?

## 2. Pain and uncertainty — 5 minutes

7. Which parts require the most professional time or attention?
8. Which parts, if any, feel repetitive?
9. Where do false positives, ambiguous morphology, or incomplete evidence create problems?
10. Are there projects where terrain review becomes difficult because of scale? What happens then?
11. What mistakes or omissions matter most, and how are they checked today?

## 3. Existing alternatives — 3–4 minutes

12. What manual visual-review, GIS, automated-terrain, commercial, or internal-script approaches
    are already used?
13. What works well about them, and what remains difficult?
14. What would make switching or adding another tool not worthwhile?

## 4. Trust and evidence — 4–5 minutes

15. What would prevent you from trusting AI-assisted terrain screening?
16. What evidence would you need before relying on it for review prioritization?
17. Would validation on geographically held-out areas matter? Why or why not?
18. What explanation, provenance, uncertainty, or audit trail would be necessary?
19. How should the system communicate false-positive burden and the risk of missed features?

## 5. Workflow fit — 3–4 minutes

Only now describe the neutral hypothesis: a bounded tool could validate authorized terrain,
prioritize terrain-pattern hypotheses, preserve machine/human evidence separately, and produce an
auditable report. It would not determine archaeology.

20. Where, if anywhere, could prioritization fit into the workflow you described?
21. Who must make the final interpretation, and what must they be able to inspect?
22. What output or report format would be useful—and what wording would be unsafe?
23. Would local/private deployment matter? What security, privacy, or integration requirements
    would decide that?

## 6. Commercial and pilot signal — final 3–4 minutes

24. If something demonstrably reduced terrain-review burden while preserving archaeologist
    control, how would your organization evaluate it?
25. Who would need to approve a bounded pilot?
26. Would your organization consider testing one? What concrete conditions would need to be met?
27. What would make a pilot valuable enough to pursue, and what would make it a clear no-go?
28. How would a tool like this normally be budgeted or procured, if at all?

Do not lead with “Would you pay £X?” A hypothetical yes is not paid willingness. Follow the
participant’s process and record decision authority, conditions, and objections.

## Close

- What important question did I fail to ask?
- May I follow up to clarify notes or share a short, non-sensitive summary?
- Should feedback be attributable, anonymized, or kept private?

Afterward, separate direct evidence from interviewer inference, record contradictory observations,
and do not mark hypotheses supported from a single positive conversation.
